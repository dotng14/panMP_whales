# Song structure in Payne and McVay (1971)

Source: Roger S. Payne and Scott McVay, “Songs of Humpback Whales,” *Science* 173, no. 3997 (13 August 1971): 585–597. The account below is their description of how the sounds are organized. The spectrograms they analyze in detail are Frank Watlington’s Bermuda recordings; Payne’s own Bermuda tapes from 1967–1971 enter the general conclusions.

## Why they call the sounds songs

Prolonged humpback vocalizations come in fixed patterns and are repeated, with considerable accuracy, every few minutes. Payne and McVay use “song” in Broughton’s median sense: a series of notes, uttered in succession and related so as to form a recognizable sequence in time. They distinguish humpback songs from typical bird songs by duration and by continuity. A bird song often lasts a few seconds and is separated from the next by silence. A humpback song lasts for minutes, and one song follows another without a significant pause or break in the rhythm of singing.

The songs they timed run from 7 minutes to more than 30 minutes. A performance is the song sung, then repeated with considerable precision. A series of such songs with no pause longer than 1 minute is a song session. Some sessions last for hours.

## The hierarchy

The paper’s terms, from the smallest sound to the whole performance, are:

**subunit < unit < phrase < theme < song < song session**

Figure 1 of the paper is a diagram of that stack.

| Level | What it is |
|---|---|
| Subunit | A single pulse or tone, resolved when the recording is slowed or examined by machine. In real time these often fuse into one impression. |
| Unit | The shortest sound they designate by ear in real time. A unit may itself be a rapid series of subunits. |
| Phrase | A series of units sung without a break. |
| Theme | An unbroken sequence of similar phrases. Within a theme the phrase may be repeated many times, and successive repeats may change gradually. |
| Song | Several distinct themes in a fixed order. |
| Song session | A series of songs with no pause longer than 1 minute between them. |

They also describe the species’ sounds, across songs, as three broad sections of material: trains of rapidly repeated pulses, often alternating with sustained tones; many short, high-frequency units, most of which rise abruptly; and lower, more sustained notes that repeat in rhythm and frequency, many of them falling in pitch. Those three kinds of material sit inside the hierarchy above. They are not extra levels.

## What stays fixed

In a song session the sequence of themes does not change. No theme is added, and none is dropped. In their sample every theme is represented by at least one phrase in every song. A phrase is occasionally incomplete or strongly modified, but the theme is still present.

The order is a cycle, not a permutation. If the themes of a song type are labeled in order, the whale sings that order and then returns to the first theme. Starting the description in the middle does not change the cycle: the sequence continues 3, 4, 5, 6, 1, 2, 3, and so on. Successive songs by the same whale keep the same theme sequence.

Different whales share this general plan. Payne and McVay do not treat the song as private to each animal. Individuals do differ in the precise shape of some units, and in how many times they repeat the phrase of a theme, but the theme order is the same.

## What is allowed to vary

Song length varies mostly because a phrase is repeated a different number of times. Two successive songs of one whale (whale III) lasted 7 minutes and 12½ minutes. In one comparison, theme 1 of that whale’s first song contained more than twice as many phrases as theme 1 of the second song. Theme 2 of whale II was about three times as long in the second song as in the first, and the spacing between its units also changed.

Phrases inside a theme are often inexact copies. They can change systematically from one repeat to the next. The paper calls this evolution of the phrase. In theme 1, a short pulse train becomes longer and higher in frequency with each repeat. In theme 3, the phrase changes in a predictable way from two loud rising sweeps to three. The number of phrases in a theme is not constant from song to song, even for the same whale in one session.

The place they mark as the start of a song is a choice of analysis, not an acoustic break. After the last phrase of the last theme, the next song begins with the first pulse train of theme 1 without a noticeable break in rhythm. The pause between the last two phrases of the final theme is, if anything, longer than the pause between the end of one song and the start of the next. A “song” might therefore be a segment of a longer cyclic performance. Wherever one begins reading, the theme order continues.

## Song types and the themes of type A

Within the shared plan, individual songs fall into a few song types. The paper works out two, called A and B, and mentions a further type C without analyzing it. Every song in a given session belongs to one type. Types A and B are alike for a long stretch and then diverge sharply.

Song type A, as sung by the whales they compare, has six themes in a fixed order.

| Theme | Form in type A |
|---|---|
| 1 | Each phrase is built on three main units. Unit 1 is a pulse train, like a motor running, with a variable number of pulses. Units 2 and 3 are a pair of sustained or wavering tones. The phrase often ends in a very low grunt. With each repeat, unit 1 grows longer and, toward the end of the theme, the pulse train tends to a higher frequency. |
| 2 | One long phrase. This is the most variable theme in the sample. The sounds are mostly ascending frequency sweeps, including high squeaks or chirps shorter than a second. There are no predictable subunits. The theme is quicker and more rhythmic than the rest of the song. Its length and the spacing of its units change markedly from song to song. |
| 3 | Phrases of one unit: a loud ascending sweep, usually lasting one to three times, followed by fainter high arpeggios. The sweep usually occurs twice at the start of the theme (once in whale III) and changes to three repeats, after which the theme stays at three. The arpeggios become more elaborate as the theme goes on, and the sounds become more sustained. This is the shortest theme in type A. |
| 4 | One phrase long. It resembles unit 1 of theme 1. Each successive unit rises and then falls, and the phrase returns to a lower frequency than the unit before it, which gives the impression of a descending series of roars or bellows. |
| 5 | Phrases typically have six units, and the theme is usually two phrases long, rarely more. Unit 1 is a downward sweep; unit 2 is a low, slow, rising or slightly warbling note, sometimes followed by one or more brief descending pulses. Units 3 and 4 are very quiet and easy to miss. Units 5 and 6 are grunts. There may be a train of grunts beyond the sixth. |
| 6 | Unit 1 is a high, loud, sustained tone. Unit 2 is a descending note, sometimes a slowly falling warble. Unit 3 is another sustained tone like unit 1. Then a descending note like unit 2, sometimes with faint pulses after unit 2 or 4 or both, and a series of one to four grunts. The theme is usually repeated ten or more times. |

Song type B follows type A through the early themes. Its theme 4 is less clearly parallel to type A. Sustained notes that stay at roughly constant pitch in type A are delivered at differing pitches in type B. The two types part company at one definite point in the form; up to that point they are strongly similar.

## One singer, one song

A song is the performance of one whale. In long recordings they never heard a stretch shorter than a full song in which themes, phrases, or units were simply missing, with pauses left where the missing piece would have been. They also found no song in which two whales alternated units or subunits to complete a pattern, echoes aside, and no case of two sources overlapping in time while one continued a song.

When two whales are both audible, each sings a complete song in its own rhythm. The songs are not locked together. Whales can be close enough to be traveling as a pair or a trio, and they can be precise about taking turns, but the turn is a whole song, not a note inside one song. The paper leaves open whether the singer is male, female, or either.

## Their summary of the structure

The structural claims in the paper’s own summary are:

1. Humpbacks produce a series of varied sounds for 7 to 30 minutes and then repeat that series with considerable precision. The performance is singing, and each repeated series is a song.
2. All prolonged sound patterns they had studied (about 50) have the same general form, and each whale keeps to its own song type.
3. There seem to be several song types. Around each type, whales construct their songs. Individual variations are pronounced. A very rough species-specific pattern remains.
4. Songs are repeated for several hours without an obvious pause between them.
5. The sequence of themes in successive songs by the same whale is the same. The number of phrases in a theme varies. In their sample, no theme is omitted.
